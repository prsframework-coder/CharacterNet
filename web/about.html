1990s: Blind to the Image

Computers in the 1990s were completely blind to what was in a picture. They could only see random colored dots, not a dog, a car, or a smiling face.

Then scientist Fei-Fei Li built ImageNet--a massive digital photo album of 14 million tagged pictures. By studying those photos, computers finally learned how to "see" the world.

2020s: Blind to the Character

AI in the 2020s could read entire libraries, but it was blind to the visual puzzle of a Chinese character. It could read a dictionary definition, but it couldn't see the little images the eye sees.

So, a 59-year-old broadcast engineer named Abraham Praise built CharacterNet--a big Lego box of China’s official 256 foundational characters. He tagged each one as a small visual brick, so that we can finally see Chinese characters visually.

I am a visual learner. I see and remember things in pictures, not in strokes. When I tried to learn Chinese the traditional way, I forgot what I learned before long. Strokes are meant for writing on paper. But puzzle pieces, like LEGO bricks, are for building images that stick in memory with zero memorization.

Look at 月 (moon). I remember it because I see 日, a window frame, through which I see the sun. And I see 儿, a pair of legs of a child, upon which my window frame stands. So 月 is the moon I see through the window frame at night.

Snap 子, 口, 儿 and 丶 together, and you have 兔 -- a rabbit. Or snap one long eyebrow 一 over two 冂 eyelids, and you get someone that is beautiful -- 丽.

Is it the official decomposition? Not always. A traditional scholar might say 月 is 冂 + 二. They are right. But I am not teaching etymology. I'm demonstrating practical recall with fun. After playing this way, I now recognize and rarely forget over 100 characters.
