Once upon a time, AI was blind.

It could read entire libraries, but a picture of a cat was just a grid of numbers. Then, a scientist named Fei-Fei Li built ImageNet--a treasure map of millions of pictures. Suddenly, AI opened its eyes and could see the world.

Years passed, and AI became super smart. But one day, a 59-year-old man sat down to learn Chinese. He looked at a character and asked DeepSeek AI, "How do I remember this?"

AI replied with the proven methods: "Write it many times over and over again. Memorize the radicals by associating them with images. Use spaced repetition drills etc."

The man blinked. Those methods were built for the classroom, but they didn't fit his brain. Once again, AI was blind. Not to images this time--but to the visual puzzle of the Chinese character right in front of its eyes. It knew the dictionary, but it couldn't see the window frame or the legs of the 月 Chinese character.

So the man said to DeepSeek, "My learning style is different. It is very visual. It is very childish." And he set out to build a new way for AI to see.

Enter CharacterNet.

Four months later, that man--Abraham Praise--had built the PRS Engine, launched the commercial platform, and created CharacterNet: an open-source dataset of the 256 foundational visual building blocks of Chinese writing.

We didn't map the ancient etymology. We mapped the visual shapes. The bricks of the language.

Designed by Abraham.

"I built this for visual learners like me. I wanted to look at a Chinese character and not see a dusty dictionary, but a box of Lego pieces. Take the pieces, snap them together, and have fun building a sticky, hilarious story around them. That is how it sticks. You are the pilot. I just built the airspace."
