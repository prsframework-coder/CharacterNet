1990s: Blind to the Image

Computers in the 1990s were completely blind to what was in a picture. They could only see random colored dots, not a dog, a car, or a smiling face. 

Then scientist Fei-Fei Li built ImageNet--a massive digital photo album of 14 million tagged pictures. By drawing bounding boxes around objects and tagging them, computers finally learned how to "see" the world.

It can finally see. ImageNet gave computers the visual vocabulary to recognize the physical world.context.

Now, AI can look at a photo and tell you a story about it. It can recognize the objects, the scene, and the context.

2020s: Blind to the Character

AI in the 2020s could read entire libraries, but it was blind to the visual puzzle of a Chinese character. When AI looks at a character, it can return its meaning and stroke order - yet it can’t see the individual visual bricks the eye actually notices.

So, a broadcast engineer named Abraham Praise built CharacterNet - a big Lego box of China’s official 256 foundational characters: the visual alphabet of the Chinese language. He tagged each one as a small visual brick, making the individual pieces easy to spot.

Now, AI sees the visual pieces of a Chinese character - and assembling them into stories becomes intuitive.

The Engineer's Proof

I see and remember things in pictures, not in strokes. When I tried to learn Chinese the traditional way, I forgot what I learned before long. Strokes are meant for writing on paper. But puzzle pieces, like LEGO bricks, are for building images that stick in memory with zero memorization.

Look at 日 (sun). I remember it because I see 日, as a window frame, through which I see the sun. And I see 儿, a pair of legs of a child, upon which my window frame stands. So 月 is the moon I see through the window frame at night, or more playfully the moon 月 is the sun 日 on legs 儿 lol.

Snap 子, 口, 儿 and 丶 together, and you have 兔 -- a rabbit. Or snap one long eyebrow 一 over two 冂 eyelids, and you get someone that is beautiful -- 丽.

Is it the official decomposition? Not always. A traditional scholar might say 月 is 冂 + 二. They are right. This isn't an etymology lesson - it's practical recall built with fun. Children naturally remember scenes built from Lego bricks, not lines repeated on paper.
